The design challenge · 1 of 7

KAIZENHow I designed it.

Audience
Small businesses without the budget for senior developers or an in-house team.
Exploration
Early versions without a character felt abstract and difficult to distinguish. Introducing Kai gave the service a name and a recognisable presence.
Decision
I redesigned the experience around Kai, from animation to the navigation logo, to create one coherent identity.

Product design, visual identity and interaction. Solo, 2025–26.

Clarity · 2 of 7

Make the service tangible.

Design challenge
Kai creates an introduction, but the visitor still needs to understand what they can buy.
Design reasoning
I use familiar work, plain language and a clear hierarchy to make the service approachable without AI knowledge.
Decision
The offer brings everyday tasks, the installation price and the payment condition into one section.

A recognisable character needs a concrete offer.

Information hierarchy · 3 of 7

Start with familiar work.

Design challenge
The service can cover many kinds of work. A broad promise alone gives visitors little to relate to.
Design reasoning
Concrete tasks give an unfamiliar service a familiar frame of reference.
Decision
I organise the examples around business activities, so visitors can connect the offer to their own working day.

Help visitors recognise a need before explaining the solution.

Expectations · 4 of 7

Make the next steps visible.

Design challenge
Interest in the service does not explain what happens after a conversation.
Design reasoning
A short sequence makes responsibilities and approval points easier to inspect.
Decision
I show four stages, from agreeing the work to reviewing it together, with a heading and explanation for each.

Agreement, delivery and review form one readable sequence.

Attention and action · 5 of 7

Give curiosity a next step.

Design intent
I want visitors to explore before asking them to make contact. Kai’s eyes invite them to notice that the page responds.
Trade-off
Kai can draw too much attention. I use his head direction to point towards the content, alongside simple copy and visual hierarchy.
Next step
The invitation asks for one task and a conversation, rather than a prepared brief.

Motion should support the message, not compete with it.

Contact · 6 of 7

Leave room for uncertainty.

Design challenge
Someone may be interested without knowing which task to start with.
Design reasoning
The contact section makes a question a valid reason to reach out.
Decision
I provide phone, email and a short form, with copy that explains there is no commitment to proceed.

A visitor should not need a finished brief to get in touch.

Learning · 7 of 7

Test beyond first impressions.

Feedback so far
Informal walkthroughs have brought positive reactions to Kai and the visual design. They were open requests for feedback, not structured usability tests.
Still unknown
I have not established whether visitors understand the offer or whether the page generates enquiries.
Next test
Ask small-business participants to explain the service, identify a relevant task and find how to make contact. Observe where Kai helps or distracts.

Positive reactions are a starting point, not proof of effectiveness.
